Weak bridges can connect groups that keep different cadences. Stronger bridges can bring those cadences together. This toy computes timing, not understanding, belonging or agreement.

What is actually being computed?

Four connected groups

256 oscillators are assigned to four groups of 64. Each group is fully connected internally: 2,016 undirected edges per group, with weight 2.4/64. The groups form a ring A–B–C–D–A. Matching member indices connect adjacent groups, giving 256 undirected bridges and exactly two bridge neighbors per person. Each bridge has weight B/2. These edges enter the differential equation; the diagram summarizes them rather than drawing all 8,320 edges.

dθᵢ/dt = ωᵢ + (2.4/64) Σⱼ∈group(i) sin(θⱼ − θᵢ)
+ (B/2) Σⱼ∈bridges(i) sin(θⱼ − θᵢ)

Natural angular speeds are seeded uniform samples from four deliberately chosen bands, in radians per model second: A 0.55–0.85; B 0.75–1.05; C 0.95–1.25; D 1.15–1.45. No frequency changes when you move the bridge slider. These bands make the question visible; they are an assumption, not a finding about people.

What to watch

R is the magnitude of the mean unit phase vector, from 0 to 1. Each dial’s hand is that group’s mean phase, with length proportional to its R. Pace is the mean instantaneous angular speed in that group. A low whole-crowd R can coexist with four highly coherent groups. A high R at one moment does not establish lasting frequency locking.

For seed 1207, with internal strength 2.4, numerical runs averaged over seconds 60–90 give mean group R ≈ 0.999 and whole-crowd R ≈ 0.468 at B = 0.08. Group mean speeds remain distinct. At B = 1.2, whole-crowd R ≈ 0.983 and the four mean speeds settle near 1.003 rad/s. These are results of this chosen model and finite test, not universal thresholds.

The original comparison

The original model is preserved: all 256 oscillators respond to the global population mean, with seeded frequencies uniform from 0.4 to 1.6 rad/s. Its slider changes K from 0 to 4. The network experiment changes both topology and frequency distribution, so this is a comparison of two models, not an isolated test of topology.

dθᵢ/dt = ωᵢ + K R sin(ψ − θᵢ)
R · exp(iψ) = (1/256) Σⱼ exp(iθⱼ)

Computation and staging

Both models use fourth-order Runge–Kutta at a fixed 1/120-second step. Internal clique sums are evaluated exactly through each group’s mean; all bridge pairs are evaluated explicitly. No reduced population model is substituted. Changing models restarts from the same seed. Replay restores that model’s initial phases and frequencies while keeping the current coupling. Nudge moves the amber-marked oscillator half a turn.

Six-frame sprite cycles show four gait cycles per oscillator turn. The footlight pulses once per turn. Human scale is fitted to doorway references at several depths, with a shared, slightly closer street-level crop. Walking paths, turns, pauses and spacing are a separate deterministic animation; they never enter either phase equation. A person can pause physically while their oscillator continues. The amber trail records eighteen model seconds. On narrow screens, routes continue beyond the crop.

The Dimes Square setting is an AI-generated illustration; the people are fictional repeated cutouts. Streets, groups, frequencies and connections are composed for exploration. This is neither a pedestrian model nor an empirical model of society. Synchronization is not inherently good or bad.

Ideas behind the experiment

Arenas, Díaz-Guilera & Pérez-Vicente (2006) show how synchronization at different time scales can reveal network communities. Brede (2008) distinguishes local from global synchronization in heterogeneous Kuramoto networks. These motivate separate measurements here; this toy reproduces neither paper’s experiment or graph.
